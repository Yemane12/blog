Finding Signal in the Noise

How to filter what matters in an age of infinite information.

Alex Chen

Writer, reader, occasional builder of things.

The problem isn't scarcity. It's abundance.

Fifty years ago, a curious person had to hunt for information — visit libraries, write letters, wait for journals. Today, the world's knowledge arrives in a firehose: 500 million tweets daily, 4 million blog posts, 720,000 hours of video uploaded every hour. The signal hasn't grown. The noise has exploded.

The Attention Economy's Tax

Every platform is designed to maximize time-on-site, not truth-discovery. Algorithms optimize for engagement — outrage, novelty, confirmation — not accuracy or depth. The loudest voices aren't the wisest; they're the most optimized.

This creates a paradox: we have more access to expertise than ever, yet it's harder to find. The expert's careful analysis drowns in a sea of hot takes. The nuanced essay loses to the thread. The signal exists, but the noise has better distribution.

"Information is not knowledge. Knowledge is not wisdom. Wisdom is not truth."

Building Your Filter

You can't consume everything. You need a personal filter — a set of heuristics that let signal through while catching noise.

1. Trust People, Not Platforms

Follow thinkers, not topics. A trusted curator's recommendation beats any algorithm. Build a network of people whose judgment you respect — then read what they read.

2. Favor Age Over Novelty

Lindy effect: the longer something has survived, the longer it will survive. A book still read after 50 years has signal. A viral thread from Tuesday likely doesn't. Default to old books, classic papers, time-tested ideas.

3. Seek Primary Sources

Secondary sources add distortion. Read the paper, not the press release. Watch the talk, not the clip. Listen to the podcast, not the highlight reel. Each layer of summarization loses nuance.

4. Limit Input Channels

You don't need Twitter, Reddit, Hacker News, LinkedIn, and five newsletters. Pick two. Go deep. The marginal value of the fifth source is negative — it adds noise without signal.

5. Create Output to Test Input

Write about what you read. Teach it. Build with it. If you can't explain an idea simply, you haven't actually received its signal — you've just cached noise.

The Discipline of Ignoring

The most powerful filter is the willingness to miss out. Most information is irrelevant to your life and work. Ignoring it isn't ignorance — it's focus.

The person who reads three great books a year understands more than the person who skims three hundred articles a week. Depth compounds. Breadth without depth is just trivia.

Signal finds you when you stop chasing noise.
